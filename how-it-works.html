How Near Me works

Near Me answers two questions: what has been said about the place you’re in, and what has been said with your name in it. It answers the first without sending anything. It answers the second by sending only your first name.

The place quote

When you tap Share Location, your browser asks for permission. If you allow it, the coordinates appear on the screen and stay there. Copy puts them on your phone’s clipboard. Nothing else happens to them.

The page already holds every place and quote it knows, in a file called places.js. Your phone picks from it:

  1. The nearest town or landmark with a quote, if one is within 30 km.
  2. Otherwise, the state or region you’re in.
  3. Otherwise, your country.
  4. Otherwise, it says “No famous quote found near you.”

To know which state or country you’re in without asking a server, the file carries a map of the world in cells a quarter of a degree on a side, about 28 km. Near a border, the answer can be one cell off.

When a place has more than one quote, Another quote shows the next one. Your phone does that too, without a request.

The name quote

Type your name and tap Get my quote. The page sends the name, and nothing else, to a small quote service.

  • The service searches Wikiquote for a line that uses the name as a whole word. Lines from films and TV come first.
  • The answer names who said it and in what work.
  • If Wikiquote finds nothing usable, fails, or takes more than 4 seconds, a short hand-checked list answers instead. Every quote on it contains its name.
  • Near Me never puts your name into a quote that doesn’t already contain it. With no real match, you see “No famous quote found for Qwzx.” with your name in place of Qwzx.
  • A word filter drops lines with profanity, slurs, or graphic violence. It is a filter, not a guarantee.

What the quote service sees and keeps

  • It accepts one thing: a name of 1 to 40 letters, spaces, hyphens, apostrophes, or periods, sent from this site.
  • It stores nothing and writes no request logs.
  • Only the first word of the name goes on to Wikiquote, as a search. Wikiquote sees the service’s address, not yours, and none of your browser’s headers. Wikimedia keeps its own logs.
  • Requests reach the service through Cloudflare.
  • The service can connect only to Wikimedia’s addresses. Each visitor can ask 10 times a minute.
  • It is one small program written in Rust, and its source is public.

What the page does on your phone

  • No account, no cookies, no analytics, and no code from anyone else.
  • The page’s security policy lets it load only its own files and talk to one address: the quote service. These site pages talk to none.
  • It sends no referrer, so the sites it links to don’t learn where you came from.
  • It saves its own files on your phone so it opens quickly. That copy holds the page, not anything about you.

Where the quotes come from

  • Wikiquote (CC BY-SA 4.0): pages about places, and lines that use names.
  • Bartlett’s Familiar Quotations, 9th edition (1905), from Project Gutenberg #27889, public domain.

Places come from Wikidata (CC0), and outlines from Natural Earth (public domain). Every place quote was approved by hand, and each one links to its source. Many places don’t have a quote yet, so you may get your state or country instead, or no quote at all.

No AI writes a quote or a citation. Quote APIs and web search weren’t used either: they would have meant sending your place to someone else.
